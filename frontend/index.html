Method

firesync counts satellite fire detections, the hot spots recorded by NASA FIRMS. It puts two generations of sensors on one scale, so a change in the calendar reflects a change on the ground and not a change of satellite.

Why the sensors need harmonizing

VIIRS pixels are smaller than MODIS pixels, so VIIRS records many more detections over the same fires. Joining the raw counts would make 2012 look like a jump in fire activity. firesync converts the MODIS Aqua record to the VIIRS scale with factors calibrated in the years both sensors flew.

Checked against INPE

Compared with the independent fire series of INPE (Programa Queimadas), the harmonized series rises and falls in the same years in all six biomes, with a monthly correlation of 0.94 to 0.99. The 2012 satellite switch creates no artificial jump in the Amazon (−0.6%), nor in the Cerrado, Caatinga and Pantanal (up to about 7%). In the Atlantic Forest (−12.7%), the ratio to INPE drifts gradually across the whole series, including after 2012, when no conversion is applied. In the Pampa (+12.3%), the result depends on years with very few fires.

Estimated and observed

January 2003 to January 2012 is estimated from MODIS Aqua and marked with hatching. From February 2012 the record is observed by VIIRS: S-NPP until October 2026, NOAA-20 from November 2026.

The uncertainty range

Each estimated month shows the median across the calibration years and the 5th to 95th percentile of those scenarios. In a leave-one-year-out test, the observed value fell inside this range in 68% to 80% of the months, depending on the biome. Observed VIIRS months carry no conversion range.

A consistent reference

Monthly anomalies and the latest complete year's comparison use the median of the reference years, rather than the mean. They use all available complete years from 2003, excluding the year being compared, so changing the displayed period does not change that year's reference. Recent days use the median of comparable windows from earlier VIIRS years.

Detections are not fires

A large fire covers many pixels and produces many detections. Only nominal and high-confidence detections of vegetation fires are counted. A month with a satellite outage is marked as missing data, which is different from a month with zero detections.

Context, not forecast

The typical range for recent days is the spread (P25 to P75) of the same dates, within 15 days, in other VIIRS years. It describes natural year-to-year variation. firesync describes the past and the present; it does not forecast fire.

Full method notes

One scale for two sensors

VIIRS sees many more hot spots than MODIS over the same area. To put both on the VIIRS scale, we compare MODIS Aqua and VIIRS S-NPP in the years both were flying (2012 to 2024). For every biome, month of the year and day or night overpass we compute a conversion factor, one per calibration year.

  • January 2003 to January 2012: estimated from MODIS Aqua × factor, shown with an uncertainty range.
  • February 2012 to October 2026: observed by VIIRS S-NPP.
  • November 2026 onward: observed by VIIRS NOAA-20.

How the alert is assessed

Normal, Attention and Critical describe recent detection activity compared with the usual level for that time of year. The alert compares the last 7 days with a seasonal reference drawn from the same dates, within 15 days, in other years.

The retrospective test evaluates weekly alerts from 2013 to 2024, then checks the following 14 days. A high-detection outcome means at least 10 VIIRS detections and a total above the seasonal 90th percentile. The comparison is a simple persistence rule: high activity this week continues into the next period. Both rules are compared at the same alert frequency.

Local-area results

Rule v1, backtested 2013–2024 on H3 resolution 4 cells (about 1,770 km²): when it flagged Critical, the next 14 days had unusually high VIIRS detections 26.6% of the time, 6.0 points more than a simple persistence rule at the same alert frequency (95% interval: 4.1 to 8.0 points, resampling whole years). The gain held in all 12 years. Under a stricter definition of a high-detection period (above the 95th percentile), the gain is small and its interval reaches zero. It measures detections, not burned area.

The levels work as a scale of recent risk. Across the test cells, the following 14 days had unusually high detections after 2.6% of Normal weeks, 10.7% of Attention weeks and 26.6% of Critical weeks, against 4.2% on average. These are past frequencies, not a forecast, and they describe the test cells on average, not each area drawn on the map. The levels do not catch everything: 52% of the high-detection periods followed a week rated Normal.

Why a simple rule and not a trained model

We also trained a machine-learning model (gradient boosting) on 13 years of fire history plus rainfall, temperature, humidity, wind and El Niño data, tested year by year on years it had not seen (2017 to 2023). With the same number of alerts, it was right only 0.9 percentage points more often than rule v1, and the weather and El Niño data did not improve it. We kept the simpler rule, which anyone can check.

Whole-biome results

Local-area performance does not carry over to a whole biome: over a whole biome, rule v1 did not beat the persistence rule in five of six biomes. So for biomes we only report how often each level occurred in 2013–2024: Amazon 8.7% of weeks Critical and 17.1% Attention; Caatinga 2.7% and 18.4%; Cerrado 3.5% and 17.8%; Atlantic Forest 4.2% and 20.4%; Pampa 10.9% and 16.0%; Pantanal 13.8% and 13.5%.

Historical example: Pantanal, 2020

The Pantanal case compares alert levels with the date of the Mato Grosso do Sul emergency decree (Decree 080/2020, official state source). With rule v1, the Pantanal reached Critical 40 days before the decree (24 July 2020) and stayed at Attention or Critical until it. In the other two known events, the continuous lead time was 20 days (Amazon, 2019) and 15 days (Pantanal, 2024). An alert before a decree indicates elevated activity before the official response; it does not show that the alert predicted a fire before it started. This case is an illustration, not statistical evidence.

Limits

The conversion assumes the ratio between the sensors in 2003 to 2011 was similar to the calibration years. This cannot be tested, because there is no VIIRS before 2012; the range measures variation between years, not this risk.

The alert and its test outcome use the same VIIRS sensor. The test measures subsequent satellite detections, not burned area. Comparison with NASA MCD64A1 burned-area data remains a next step. The seasonal reference excludes the evaluated year but includes later years, so this is a retrospective assessment, not a historical forecast using only information available at the time.

The intensity class (low, medium, high) helps prioritize where to look, but it does not replace field information, wind or the advance of the fire front.
